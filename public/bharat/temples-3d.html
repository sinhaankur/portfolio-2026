    How it was built

      The books

        The books behind the buildings

        Builders in India wrote down their craft: how to read a site, face a temple, measure it and choose its stone. These are some of the treatises that survive. For most temples here, no record says which book, if any, the builders used, so each temple’s panel says what is known and leaves the rest as a gap.

        Mayamata

        South India

        How to choose a site, face a building, size it and choose its materials; still respected by traditional builders (sthapatis) of the south.

        Mayamata, ed. and tr. Bruno Dagens (IGNCA)

        Samarangana Sutradhara

        Malwa, credited to King Bhoja (11th c.)

        Survey of land and soil, the system of measurement, site plans, and temples from plan to finial.

        K. F. Sompura, Structural Temples of Gujarat: what the Samarangana covers

        Aparajitaprccha

        Gujarat, under Bhima II (1178 to 1240)

        A large Sanskrit treatise of the western Indian tradition.

        SOAS paper, Architectural Science in Jain Poetry: the Aparajitaprccha and the Vastusara

        Shilpa Prakasha

        Odisha, medieval

        Temple parts, iconography and symbolism, and the diagrams (yantras) laid under plans and images.

        Shilpa Prakasha, tr. Alice Boner and Sadasiva Rath Sarma

        Vastusara

        Thakkura Pheru, 1315

        Houses, Jain images and temples, written in Prakrit as a working handbook.

        SOAS paper, Architectural Science in Jain Poetry: the Aparajitaprccha and the Vastusara

        Prasadamandana and others

        Mandana, Mewar, under Rana Kumbha

        Treatises by the head builder of the Mewar court.

        Jaina Art and Architecture, vol. 2: Mandana and his treatises under Rana Kumbha

        Tantrasamuccaya

        Kerala, 1428

        Rituals and rules for building a temple; still used as the reference in Kerala.

        A. K. V. Arjun, Revisiting Tantrasamuccaya (IJFMR)

        Masculine, feminine and neuter stone

        Some of these books sort stone into masculine, feminine and neuter by its look and sound. In Mandana’s Devatamurti-prakarana, a masculine stone is one colour, dense and smooth, and rings like a bell; a feminine stone is thick at the base and slim at the tip and rings like a bronze cymbal; a neuter stone is slim at the base and silent. Masculine stone was for lingas and gods, feminine for pedestals and goddesses, neuter for foundation stones, the base of the temple and tanks. Which stones in any one temple were chosen this way is not recorded.

        Mandana, Devatamurti-prakarana: masculine, feminine and neuter stone

        How the stone was carved

        What is known about the carving itself comes mostly from three places: carvers still at work, carvings left unfinished, and the measuring rules in the old texts. These notes are general; they are not claims about any one temple above.

        Draw on the stone first

        Carvers paint their guides straight onto the block: red lines for a grid, black lines for the outline of the form. The Ministry of Textiles craft page for Mamallapuram describes the sketch being made in red oxide.

        T4Art of Making in Antiquity, King’s College London: carver’s guides, Mamallapuram workshops

        T2Development Commissioner (Handicrafts): Mahabalipuram stone sculpture

        One small tool does most of the work in granite

        In the Mamallapuram workshops the point chisel is used at almost every stage, from roughing out to finishing. The chisels are short and blunt quickly on granite, so a pile of them sits by the carver to be sharpened again and again. The carvers work sitting or crouching. A flat chisel, the palamunai uli, levels surfaces.

        T4Art of Making in Antiquity: the point chisel, Mamallapuram

        T4Art of Making in Antiquity: short chisels, often sharpened

        Unfinished work shows the order

        Carvings left half done, in rock-cut halls and on loose slabs across the subcontinent, show the order of work: block, rough shape, then detail. Vidya Dehejia and Peter Rockwell, a sculptor, studied them for The Unfinished: Stone Carvers at Work on the Indian Subcontinent (2016). The Sarnath capital in the model goes through the same three steps, drawn by eye.

        T4Dehejia and Rockwell, The Unfinished (contents and opening pages)

        Hard stone and soft stone

        Granite takes the point chisel slowly. The Hoysala temples are built of chloritic schist, which takes very fine detail. It is often said to be soft when quarried and to harden in the air; that claim is repeated widely, but we have not found it in a study of the stone, so it is not used here as fact.

        T2UNESCO: Sacred Ensembles of the Hoysalas (built with chloritic schist)

        Proportion: the books and the carvings

        The texts set out a system of proportion, talamana, counted in tala (a palm or face length) and angula (a finger’s breadth). When Gift Siromoney measured Pallava and early Chola sculptures, he found that the texts mix two systems, and that the carvings do not simply follow one rule. A 2026 dataset from IIT Gandhinagar tests the same question on Badami Chalukya and Hoysala sculpture using laser scans.

        T4Gift Siromoney, iconometry of Pallava sculpture (Chennai Mathematical Institute archive)

        T1Poorva Salvi, Iconometry of Badami Chalukya and Hoysala Sculptures (dataset, 2026)
